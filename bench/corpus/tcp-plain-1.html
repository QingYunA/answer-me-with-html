TCP Connection Lifecycle

The three-way handshake and the four-way teardown

Why TCP needs setup and teardown

TCP provides a reliable, ordered byte stream. To do that, both endpoints must agree on starting sequence numbers, window sizes and options before any data flows, and must agree when each side is finished sending. The handshake establishes that shared state; the teardown releases it cleanly.

Key header flags: SYN (synchronize sequence numbers), ACK (acknowledgment field is valid), FIN (sender has no more data), RST (abort the connection).

1. Three-way handshake (connection establishment)

Step by step

  1. SYN – The client picks an initial sequence number (ISN) x and sends a segment with SYN set. It enters SYN_SENT.
  2. SYN-ACK – The server, listening, picks its own ISN y, acknowledges the client's SYN with ack = x+1, and sets both SYN and ACK. It enters SYN_RECEIVED.
  3. ACK – The client acknowledges the server's SYN with ack = y+1. Both sides are now ESTABLISHED; the third segment may already carry data.

Why three and not two?

Each side must both announce its ISN and have it acknowledged. The server's SYN and ACK are combined in one segment, giving three messages. Two would leave the server unable to confirm the client received its ISN, and would let stale duplicate SYNs from old connections open half-valid connections.

The SYN segments also negotiate options such as MSS, window scaling, SACK and timestamps. SYN and FIN each consume one sequence number, which is why the acknowledgment is x+1.

Security note: a SYN flood sends many SYNs without completing step 3, filling the server's half-open queue. SYN cookies defend against this by encoding state in the ISN instead of allocating memory.

2. Four-way teardown (connection termination)

A TCP connection is full-duplex: each direction is a separate stream and is closed independently. Closing both directions takes a FIN and an ACK per direction, so four segments (the middle two can merge into one if the server has nothing left to send, giving three).

Step by step

  1. FIN – The side that finishes first (the active closer) sends FIN and enters FIN_WAIT_1. It will send no more data but can still receive.
  2. ACK – The peer acknowledges the FIN and enters CLOSE_WAIT. The active closer moves to FIN_WAIT_2. The peer's application is told the stream ended and may keep sending.
  3. FIN – When the peer's application calls close, it sends its own FIN and enters LAST_ACK.
  4. ACK – The active closer acknowledges, entering TIME_WAIT. The peer receives it and goes straight to CLOSED.

TIME_WAIT

The active closer waits 2×MSL (maximum segment lifetime; commonly 60 s on Linux) before fully closing. This ensures that:

  • the final ACK can be retransmitted if it was lost and the peer resends its FIN;
  • delayed segments from this connection expire and cannot be mistaken for part of a new connection using the same address/port pair.

Many connections stuck in CLOSE_WAIT usually means the local application is not calling close(). Many in TIME_WAIT is normal on busy clients and servers that close first.
